Chapter 3: Wave Functions

In the previous chapter, we established that physical states in quantum mechanics are represented by vectors in a complex Hilbert space. However, for a single particle moving in one dimension, it is often more convenient to represent its state not by an abstract vector, but by a complex-valued function of position, denoted as Ψ(x,t).

Born's Statistical Interpretation

The quantity |Ψ(x,t)|² dx represents the probability of finding the particle between x and x + dx at time t.

Consequently, the wave function itself is completely determined by the Schrödinger equation, but its physical meaning only emerges when we consider its squared magnitude. This profound shift from deterministic trajectories to probabilistic outcomes marks the fundamental departure of quantum mechanics from classical physics.

chat_bubble

The normalization condition requires that the total probability of finding the particle somewhere in space must be exactly 1. Mathematically, this is expressed as the integral of |Ψ|² over all space equating to 1.

If a wave function is initially normalized, does it stay normalized as time evolves? The answer is crucial for the consistency of the theory, and fortunately, it is yes, provided the potential energy function is real. This property is known as the conservation of probability.

Think of it like a game of hide-and-seek. The particle is definitely hiding *somewhere* in the universe.

  • arrow_right_alt |Ψ|² gives you the probability of finding it in a specific small spot.
  • arrow_right_alt If you add up (integrate) the probabilities of finding it in *every possible spot* across all of space, it must equal 100% (which is 1 mathematically).

If it didn't equal 1, it would mean the particle has a chance of existing nowhere, or existing more than 100%, which breaks the rules of reality!
